# One-Line Office Jokes — candidates

Judge criteria (in the judge's own priority order), each scored /5, total /25:

1. A single-line joke about office life (not a story, slogan or list)
2. Turns on a real detail of office life (meetings, email, printer, coffee machine)
3. Lands its laugh in the last few words
4. Clear on one reading, no setup that needs explaining
5. Fresh wording, not a joke already going round the internet

## Candidates

### 1. "Our printer only jams on documents marked urgent, which is how I know it can read." — 24/25
1. 5 — one line, one joke, office life. 2. 5 — the printer, the classic office saboteur. 3. 5 — the turn lands in the last three words ("it can read"). 4. 5 — instantly clear. 5. 4 — personifying the printer is a familiar move, but "it can read" as the punch is not a circulating meme.

### 2. "I hit reply-all to ask everyone to stop hitting reply-all, and now forty-seven of us are apologising in unison." — 23/25
1. 5. 2. 5 — reply-all, a real office wound. 3. 5 — "apologising in unison" is a strong final beat. 4. 5. 5. 3 — reply-all jokes are well-trodden; the wording is fresh but the premise is not.

### 3. "Our coffee machine has two settings — burnt and out of order — and somehow both taste like a deadline." — 23/25
1. 5. 2. 5 — the coffee machine. 3. 4 — "taste like a deadline" lands, but the middle does the work. 4. 5. 5. 4 — fresh image, though "burnt office coffee" is a common complaint.

### 4. "My calendar invited me to a meeting about my calendar, so I declined: I was already in a meeting." — 24/25
1. 5. 2. 5 — calendar and meetings, the office's native habitat. 3. 5 — the last clause is the whole joke. 4. 5. 5. 4 — meeting-about-meetings is a known trope, but the self-inviting calendar twists it.

### 5. "Hot-desking means I spend twenty minutes a day hunting for a chair, a charger, and a colleague who remembers my name." — 22/25
1. 4 — it leans towards a list (chair, charger, colleague). 2. 5 — hot-desking is a real detail. 3. 4 — the last item is the funniest, but the list dilutes the final beat. 4. 4 — three items to track in one line. 5. 4.

### 6. "The printer jammed, so IT opened a ticket, and the ticket got stuck in the printer queue." — 24/25
1. 5. 2. 5 — printer plus IT ticket queue, two real details in one line. 3. 5 — "stuck in the printer queue" is a clean final twist. 4. 5. 5. 4 — the circular-bureaucracy joke is a familiar shape, freshly dressed.

### 7. "I put 'on annual leave' in my out-of-office and my inbox scheduled three urgent meetings to welcome me back." — 20/25
1. 5. 2. 4 — out-of-office and inbox are real, but the joke is about the inbox behaving, which is a stretch. 3. 3 — the laugh is spread across the line, no sharp final beat. 4. 4. 5. 4.

### 8. "The Monday meeting could have been an email, but the email would have needed a meeting to explain it." — 21/25
1. 5. 2. 5 — Monday meeting, the judge's own example. 3. 4 — the turn arrives at the end, but it is a logical twist rather than a laugh. 4. 5. 5. 2 — "this meeting could have been an email" is one of the most repeated office lines on the internet.

## Winner

Candidate 1, tied at 24/25 with candidates 4 and 6. It wins the tie because:

- It is the only one of the three whose punch is a single surprising idea ("the printer can read") rather than a situational twist — the laugh is in the last three words, exactly what the judge rewards most after the office detail.
- It needs no office jargon beyond "urgent documents": candidate 4 needs the reader to hold "calendar invited me" as a premise, and candidate 6 needs the IT ticket system; candidate 1's setup is one clause.
- Its detail is the most universal: every office worker has met the printer that only fails when it matters.

## Revision

Draft: "Our printer only jams on documents marked urgent, which is how I know it can read."

Final: "Our printer only jams on urgent documents, which is how I know it can read."

What changed: "documents marked urgent" became "urgent documents" — two words cut, no meaning lost. The setup is shorter, so the punch ("it can read") arrives sooner and the line reads as one breath. 86 characters → 75 characters, still a single line, still inside the 1–199 character gate.
